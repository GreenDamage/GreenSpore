Cyclocybe aegerita

Pioppino

A rich, earthy mushroom that grows in dense clusters on poplar wood — also known as the black poplar mushroom, prized for its firm texture and nutty flavor.

What it is

Pioppino grows in tight, dense clusters of slender caps and long stems, ranging from dark chestnut brown when young to a lighter tan as they mature. The name comes from the Italian word for poplar ("pioppo"), the tree it's most associated with in the wild.

It's a white-rot fungus, meaning it breaks down both the lignin and cellulose in wood, leaving behind a soft, bleached residue. In nature it fruits on dead and dying poplar, willow, and elm, usually at the base of trunks or on buried roots. Its firm texture holds up well to cooking, which is part of why it's popular in cultivation.

Family
Strophariaceae
Habitat
Poplar & willow wood
Feeding type
White-rot saprotroph
Growth style
Dense clusters

The life cycle

Spore release

A mature Pioppino cap releases spores from gills on its underside. Because the mushrooms fruit in dense clusters, a single cluster can release a large volume of spores at once, drifting off in search of exposed poplar or willow wood.

Germination

A spore landing on suitable wood germinates and sends out a hypha. As with other mushrooms, that single thread needs to fuse with a compatible hypha from another spore before real growth can begin.

Mycelium

The fused mycelium spreads through the wood as a white-rot organism, breaking down both lignin and cellulose. This slowly bleaches and softens the wood as the network expands and stores up energy for fruiting.

Primordia

Once the substrate is fully colonized and triggered by a change in conditions — typically cooler temperatures and higher humidity — the mycelium forms clusters of small primordia rather than single pins, setting up the dense bouquets Pioppino is known for.

Fruiting body

The primordia expand into slender-stemmed, dark brown-capped mushrooms, growing in a tight cluster from a shared base. Caps lighten as they open and mature, and the cluster is typically harvested whole at once.

Sporulation

As the caps fully open, their gills release a new generation of spores and the cycle repeats. In cultivation, this stage overlaps with peak harvest — clusters are cut before the caps flatten and spore drop is heaviest.
