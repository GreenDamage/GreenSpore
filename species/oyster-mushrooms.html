Pleurotus ostreatus

Oyster Mushrooms

A shelf-shaped, wood-loving mushroom named for its resemblance to the sea oyster — and one of the fastest, most forgiving fungi to grow.

What they are

Oyster mushrooms grow in overlapping clusters, or "shelves," on the sides of dead and dying hardwood trees. Each cap fans out from an off-center stem, running from soft grey to deep charcoal in the wild, with pink, yellow, and golden varieties bred for cultivation.

They're saprotrophs — organisms that feed on dead organic matter rather than living plants. In nature, that means fallen logs and stumps. In cultivation, it means straw, sawdust, and other agricultural byproducts, which is part of why oysters are such an efficient crop: they turn waste material into food.

Family
Pleurotaceae
Habitat
Dead hardwood
Feeding type
Saprotrophic
Growth style
Shelf clusters

The life cycle

Spore release

A mature mushroom releases millions of microscopic spores from gills on the underside of its cap. Carried by air currents, most land somewhere with no chance of growing — but a few reach a hospitable, moist surface with the right food source nearby.

Germination

A spore that lands somewhere favorable germinates, sending out a fine thread called a hypha. On its own, a single hypha can't reproduce — it needs to meet and fuse with a compatible hypha from another spore.

Mycelium

Once two compatible hyphae fuse, the resulting network grows outward as mycelium — a web of fine white threads that spreads through its food source (straw, sawdust, or wood), digesting it externally and absorbing the nutrients.

Primordia (pinning)

When the mycelium has fully colonized its substrate and conditions shift — a drop in temperature, a rise in humidity, fresh air exchange — it responds by forming tiny knots of tissue called primordia, or "pins." This is the first visible sign a mushroom is coming.

Fruiting body

Pins that get enough humidity and airflow expand rapidly into full mushrooms — the fan-shaped caps and off-center stems we recognize and harvest. This is the fruiting body: a temporary structure whose only job is to make spores.

Sporulation

As the cap matures, its gills release a new generation of spores into the air, and the cycle begins again. In cultivation, this stage also marks harvest time — mushrooms are picked at their peak, just as sporulation begins.
